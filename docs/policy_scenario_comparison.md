## Critical assumptions

Every result is a range: the model runs 1,000 times, each with different plausible values for what it is
unsure about. For each reform we measured how much of that range each uncertain assumption explains on its
own, using the runs behind the published results (`reports/range-drivers/`).

**In Minnesota, the benchmark state:**

| Reform | How wide the range is (P90 ÷ P10) | What drives it |
|---|---|---|
| Missing middle | about ×40 | **How readily owners and builders use a new 2–4 home allowance** (the uptake rate fitted to places that already allow it): 96% |
| Fee cut (the Challenge's $50,000 → $25,000) | about ×12 | **How strongly building responds to lower fees** (the elasticity): 74%. How much of the cut landowners capture: 5% |
| Fee cut (half of real local fees) | about ×11 | The elasticity: 58%. City-by-city fee levels and other factors: 18% |
| ADUs | about ×4.5 | **How fast owners add ADUs once allowed** (fitted uptake rate): 38%. **How much a partial ADU rule already counts:** 29% (these two overlap, because one is refitted for each value of the other). Legalized or replacement units: 10%. Homes that would be built elsewhere in the state anyway: 6% |

**The other states look the same:**
- The missing-middle uptake rate explains 96% everywhere.
- The fee elasticity explains 92–93% (42–53% in Oregon, where fees vary most between cities).
- For ADUs, more of the range comes from place-to-place differences and which local rules apply: 28–33%
  in Ohio, Texas and Oregon. In Wisconsin, a state the model was not fitted on, 68% comes from its
  unseen-state adjustment.

**What this means for a decision:**
- The missing-middle estimate rests almost entirely on one number: how much 2–4 home allowances get used
  where they already exist. A state adopting this reform should watch early permit counts closely. Those
  counts will narrow the range faster than anything else.
- For fee cuts, the evidence on how builders respond to cost changes matters far more than the fee amounts
  themselves.
- Assumptions about construction costs, completion rates and how much new housing simply moves between
  places matter much less: under 10% each.

Each share is the assumption's effect on its own. Effects that come only from two assumptions combining are
not separated out.
