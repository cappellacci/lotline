Lotline

Lotline is a tool to help understand how much new housing specific housing reforms would add. It learns from places in the country where these reforms have passed, takes each state's housing market and local factors into account, and estimates what a reform would add over five years, as a range.

    The data

    What the model needs, and from which years

    Every estimate starts from the same question: how many lots could take an extra home under the reform, and how often have owners actually built on lots like them? Answering it takes seven kinds of data.

    The time line. The model learns from permits through 2025, the last full year of data, and forecasts the five years 2027–2031, when a reform passed in 2027 could act. 2026 falls in between and is not reported. A few places' most recent years are hidden from the model so its forecasts for them can be tested blind.

    The model

    How we got from the question to the numbers, one step at a time: what we measure, how the data were prepared, the three reform models side by side, where the range comes from and what we ruled out, how we tested it, and how it carries over to a new state.

    1

    The goal: exactly what we measure

    The question. If a state passed one of three reforms, how many more homes would it gain over the five years 2027–2031 than under the rules already in place? The headline counts net new housing: homes finished, minus houses torn down to build them and homes that would have been built elsewhere in the state anyway.

    • The three reforms follow the Challenge Handbook to the letter.
      • ADUs: one backyard home per house, at least 600 sq ft, with no parking required.
      • Missing middle: 2–6 homes allowed on lots now zoned for one house, up to three stories.
      • Fees: development fees cut by half, shown two ways: the Challenge benchmark (a $50,000 fee everywhere, cut to $25,000) and each city's real fees cut by half.
    • The comparison is always reform against no reform. "No reform" keeps every city's own rules, including laws already passed that take effect by the end of 2027. A reform is never credited with homes that current law will produce anyway.
    • The unit is homes in building permits, added up place by place to a state total. That total becomes two numbers:
      • all new homes: permits that get finished and add a home someone can live in;
      • net new housing: all new homes minus those that would have been built elsewhere in the state anyway. That share is a quarter by default, anywhere from none to half.
    • Every answer is a range. The model runs 1,000 times, each with different plausible values for everything it is unsure about. The page shows the middle 80% of those runs (P10 to P90) and the midpoint.

    2

    Preparing the data

    For every city, town and township in every year, the model needs two counts: how many lots a reform could touch, and how many homes were permitted. The Data page lists every source. These are the rules we applied to them.

    • Lots the reform could touch. One written rule, applied the same way in every state.
      • For ADUs: a lot with one single-family house.
      • For missing middle: a lot zoned for single-family homes only, holding one house or vacant.
      Where a place has no parcel records, Census counts of single-family homes stand in. We tested that stand-in against real parcel counts in all five states, and it came within 8% everywhere (0.92 to 1.07 times the parcel count). For missing middle it runs about 12% low, a known gap.
    • Homes permitted. Census permit counts by building size (1, 2, 3–4 and 5+ homes) for every place since 1992, plus city permit files that mark ADUs. A project total repeated on several permits is counted once.
    • Local rules, with dates. For each city we looked up when it allowed ADUs or missing middle, and how strict the rule is: by right or not, whether the owner must live there, parking, size. Places nobody has coded borrow the rules of similar coded places of the same size.
    • Unknown is never zero. A missing year stays missing; it does not count as a year with no building. An unknown zoning code counts the lot as eligible, flagged as an assumption.
    • Hidden years for blind tests. Some places' recent records were locked away from the model so we could predict them first and look later:
      • Oregon from 2018: predicted from the other four states, then opened (November 5) and added to the fit;
      • Charlotte from mid-2023, including the county figures its permits are filed under: predicted, then opened (November 9);
      • St. Paul from 2024: kept locked until its frozen prediction was scored (16 November 2026).
    • Data tiers. Each state is labelled by what its data rests on:
      • tier 1: local parcels with year built;
      • tier 2: local parcels with land use;
      • tier 3: national data only.

    3

    What each model does, and how we get our ranges

    3a. What gets built anyway

    Each place keeps its own recent pace: homes permitted per 1,000 eligible lots over 2021–2025. Small places with lumpy records are pulled toward their county's pace. On top of that runs the state's building cycle. The model replays year-to-year swings drawn from the state's last 25 years, so some runs see a boom and others a bust. This baseline is what every reform is measured against.

    3b. Why we built each effect from its parts

    Our first plan was to compare reformed cities with similar cities that did not reform. In our four states those comparisons could not tell a real effect from noise: they could only have detected effects many times larger than any plausible one. So each reform's effect is built from its working parts, and each part is measured separately:

    extra homes = lots the reform opens up × share where building pays × how often owners build, once it pays

    • Lots the reform opens up counts only what the reform adds beyond a city's current rule. A city that already allows ADUs gains little.
    • Share where building pays compares, lot by lot, what a new home would sell or rent for with what it costs to build, finance and sell.
    • How often owners build is measured from our own reformed places for ADUs and missing middle, not borrowed from other studies. Fee cuts are the exception: no place in our states cut fees on the scale of the reform, so their response comes from published studies (see the fee card).
    • What is already being built is never credited to the reform.

    3c. The three models, in depth

    Each reform has its own model, built on the same frame. Pick one to read it: every card answers the same five questions, then goes state by state.

    In one sentence

    Every house lot where an ADU is allowed has a small yearly chance of getting one; the chance depends on the city's rules and on whether it is a big city or a suburb, and the reform raises it where local rules hold ADUs back.

    How it works

    ADUs a year = lots where allowed × base chance × how much the local rule allows × big-city factor × place's own luck

    • Base chance: about 2 lots in 10,000 a year get an ADU in a suburb whose rules match the reform (0.22 per 1,000 lots; 0.17 to 0.30).
    • Local rules: a rule whose only extra condition is that the owner lives there gets the full chance. A rule with other limits (a parking space, a smaller size cap, a permit hearing) reaches about 70% of it. That share is uncertain, so each run draws it, from 40% to 100%.
    • Big cities and suburbs. Each place gets one of three labels, from national census data only:
      • core city: the biggest city in its metro area, or one at least a third its size;
      • suburb: any other place in a metro area;
      • town: a place outside any metro area.
      Core cities build ADUs at about 6 times the rate of suburbs with the same rules (3 to 11 times). This one fact explains much of the gap between states.
    • Place's own luck. Some places build more or fewer ADUs than their rules suggest, for reasons we can't see: local builders, prices, habits. The model does not guess why; it gives each place a multiplier drawn to match how much real places differ. This is a large part of why answers come as ranges.
    • Prices and borrowing. The chance rises where an ADU's rent covers what it costs to build, and with homeowners' equity; it falls as mortgage rates rise, because most ADUs are built by owners who borrow against their house. The sizes of these effects are judgment calls with wide ranges.
    • Places that count their own ADUs keep their own recent pace as the no-reform rate. The reform adds the gap between what today's rule allows and what the reform allows. A place that allows no ADUs today gets the full rate for its type.
    What it learned from
    • Counted ADUs: city permit files that mark ADUs, in places across Minnesota, North Carolina, Texas, Oregon and Ohio.
    • Checked local rules: for 70 places we went back through their ordinances year by year and recorded their ADU rules from 2016 to 2025, then checked those records against the original sources. The fit uses 498 place-years.
    • Tested and set aside: a slower start in the first years after a city first allows ADUs. The data leaned that way, but it did not predict held-out places any better, so it was not used.
    How we tested it

    Pass

    We hid one state, fitted the model on the others and predicted the hidden state from two things only: its places' types and the coded rules of its 20 largest places. A prediction counted as useful if the actual number fell inside its range (P10 to P90) and the midpoint was within a factor of two.

    Useful in all four. Ohio could not be tested this way: only 3 of its places have usable ADU counts. These four states also shaped the model's design, so this is not a fully blind test. The blind one, Oregon predicted before any of its data was opened, missed low (see the tests table below).

    In one sentence

    When a place starts allowing 2–4 homes on lots zoned for one house, small multi-unit buildings rise above the place's usual pace, slowly, and mostly where rents cover the cost of building.

    How it works

    2–4 unit buildings a year = the place's usual pace + mature rate × eligible lots × how much the rule changed × whether building pays × how far in the reform is

    • The usual pace is the place's own 2–4 unit building in the 3 years before the reform, moved with its growth in other homes.
    • The mature rate: about 1 new 2–4 unit building per 1,000 eligible lots a year once the reform is fully in effect where building just pays. Its range is very wide (0.13 to 19 per 1,000).
    • How far in the reform is: the effect builds up over time, reaching half its full size after about 4 years (1 to 6), as in Oregon's places that the state law required to allow it. By the fifth year it is still growing.
    • Lumpy years: a third or more of these buildings come in projects of three or more, so a place's count swings with whether one large project lands in the window.
    • What it counts: Census permits for 2–4 unit buildings, the only size reported the same way everywhere. The reform allows up to 6, so this is a floor.
    What it learned from
    • 93 places in all five states (86 of them in Oregon, most under its 2019 law, HB 2001) whose coded rule raised the number of homes allowed, or how easily they are approved, between 2013 and 2025.
    • Places that always allowed missing middle, to anchor the mature rate (widened, because such places may differ).
    • 483 other places, used only to measure how lumpy a place's years are.
    • Tested and not used: place types (they did not narrow the ranges).
    How we tested it

    Partial

    • Single places: 78% of 136 places landed inside their range (the target was 60% to 95%).
    • A new state: with Oregon left out, the other states predicted 457 to 4,789 buildings in Oregon's places (midpoint 915); 703 were built.
    • Blind: Charlotte passed, with the earlier values. St. Paul, frozen with these values before any result was read, missed: 55 buildings in 2024–2025 against a range of 9 to 28. It built 33 in its first full year, against about 6 a year before.
    • The weak spot: the reform's own effect is not clearly detected. The model with a reform effect beats the same model without one by only about 1.5 standard errors, short of the 2 we set as the bar, and a fake reform date three years early shows a larger effect than the real dates.

    What it can't tell you
    • How big the effect is. The counts are consistent with an effect but cannot pin it down, so the mature rate's range spans about 150-fold.
    • 5–6 unit buildings, which Census permits lump with larger ones.
    • Height and floor-area limits, which can stop a fourplex the zoning allows.

    In one sentence

    Cutting the fees a builder pays the city lowers the cost of a new home; part of the saving goes to landowners, and building responds weakly to the rest.

    How it works

    extra homes = usual homes × how strongly building responds × builder's share of the cut ÷ total cost of the home, plus lots that newly pay

    • The cut, shown two ways on the Results page: the Challenge benchmark, $25,000 a home (half of a $50,000 fee assumed everywhere), and half of each city's real fees for houses, duplexes and fourplexes. Buildings of 5 or more homes are never covered.
    • Builder's share: about half of a fee cut ends up in land prices, so builders keep the other half (a quarter to three quarters).
    • How strongly building responds: a 10% cut in cost raises building by about 3% (Baum-Snow and Han 2024, measured neighborhood by neighborhood), from almost none (Mayer and Somerville 2000; Campbell and Alm 2006) to 17% (the metro-wide response in Saiz 2010 and Aastveit and others 2020). Each run draws one value.
    • Lots that newly pay: where parcel data allow, the cost model also counts lots that cross from not paying to paying after the cut.
    What it learned from
    • Fee schedules coded for 58 places, which hold about a third of each state's usual building. Other places take their state's median fee ($3,900 a home in Texas to $9,100 in Minnesota).
    • How strongly building responds comes from published studies, not our own places: most studies of fees find no clear effect on how much is built, and the high end comes from metro-wide supply studies.
    How we tested it

    Not yet run

    The fee model is built and checked against hand-worked examples, but it has not been tested against what happened after a real fee change. Its results are on the Results page as a range, like the others.

    What it can't tell you
    • How a city replaces the money. Fees often pay for roads, pipes and parks; what happens to those is not modelled.
    • Its own accuracy. Unlike the other two models, its key number is borrowed, not measured here.
    • Oregon: only 4 cities are coded (Bend, Eugene, Portland and Salem), with fees far above the other states' ($17,500 to $42,800 a home, mostly system development charges). Four is too few for an Oregon median, so its other places take the median of every coded place (about $7,100), which likely understates what they charge.

    Sources: docs/analysis_plan.md D-010, D-065, D-073, D-076, D-077; config/params/fees.yaml.

    3f. Where the range comes from

    • The building cycle: each run replays a different stretch of the state's past booms and busts.
    • The measured rates: how often owners build, and how fast, are drawn from their fitted ranges, not fixed at one value.
    • How much a partial local rule already allows (ADUs): each run draws it from the values the data support.
    • Place-to-place differences we cannot measure: each place gets its own multiplier, drawn to match how much real places differ. A state the model was not fitted on gets a wider spread still.
    • Judgment calls are drawn from stated low-to-high ranges, each with a source in config/params:
      • finished homes per permit;
      • homes that would have been built elsewhere;
      • conversions of existing units.
    • Year-to-year chance: even at a known rate, the count in any one year varies.

    Carrying it to another state

    Nothing in the model is written for one state. A new state is one settings file plus whatever data it has.

    • What a state supplies: its Census code and any local data it has (parcel rolls, city permit files, coded local rules, fee schedules). Everything national (Census permits, housing counts, prices, rates) is fetched automatically.
    • With little local data:
      • lots come from Census housing counts, adjusted using the stand-in test above;
      • uncoded places borrow rules from similar coded places;
      • the state's unknown character gets the widest spread the model has, anywhere from a third of a typical state to three times one.
    • ADU place types come from national data, so every place in any state gets one, even in a state with no ADU counts at all.
    • Places outside a state ADU law borrow the rules of small towns in states without one, not those of big cities that have reformed.
    • The test case is Oregon. It was predicted from the other four states before its own results were looked at, and the app shows it as the "transfer" state. Its missing-middle results were not used to fit the model. Since 2026-11-06, Portland and Bend are part of the ADU fit.

    !

    What the model cannot tell you

    • Single cities are very uncertain. The state total is the number to rely on; one city's range can be very wide.
    • Missing middle's effect is not clearly detected: its edge over "no effect" is about 1.5 standard errors, short of the 2 we set as the bar, so its range is very wide.
    • High-uptake ADU markets can be under-predicted, as Portland and Bend were in the blind test.
    • Not modelled:
      • how two reforms interact on the same lot: each reform is run on its own;
      • 5–6 unit buildings, which Census permits lump with larger ones;
      • height and floor-area limits;
      • approval times;
      • what happens to prices, rents, or who lives in the new homes.
    • Fee cuts are not tested against a real fee change, and their key number (how strongly building responds) is borrowed from published studies.
