Lotline · D-089 ADU state profiles · 14 Nov 2026

Every state's ADU forecast leans on places unlike the ones the model learned from, and towns are not the reason

D-089 profiled every jurisdiction in the five states by type (core city, suburb, town), size, price and owner share, and set each state against the 64 places the ADU model was fitted on. Towns, the one type with no data, hold 9–30% of each state's lots but yield only 1–5% of its expected ADUs, so the town prior barely moves any range. The bigger gap is price and size: the fitted suburbs are the pricey Twin Cities and Triangle ones, and most of Ohio's and Texas's places are cheaper or bigger than anything fitted.

Finding 2

Most lots in every state are outside the fit's evidence, mainly on price and size

A place is outside the evidence if it is a town, or if its home value, rent, owner share or lot count lies outside the 5th–95th percentile of the fitted places of its type. The fitted suburbs all have median home values of $228k or more and rents of $1,114 or more (2025 dollars); many Ohio and Texas suburbs are cheaper. Texas's biggest cities and suburbs are also bigger than any fitted place.

Finding 3

Towns hardly matter to the totals; extrapolation does

Each state's ADU permits under its current rules, 2027-2031, from the scenario's own inputs (eligible lots, rules, rent and value, the observed floor) with Form A + place types. Towns add few ADUs because their rents are low and fewer of them allow ADUs, so treating them as suburbs or giving them the wide prior changes P90 by under 3%. What does matter is the share of each total that comes from places outside the evidence: from a quarter in North Carolina to nearly all of Texas.

What it means

Label the ranges, and look for cheaper suburbs before towns

By the rule set in advance, every state's ADU range gets the label "mostly outside the places the model was fitted on". The town prior can go to config as written; it costs nothing and closes the one unmeasured type. The more useful next data would be ADU counts for a few lower-priced suburbs (Ohio or Texas), the places the forecasts lean on most and the model has never seen.
