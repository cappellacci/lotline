What happens if local governments cut their development fees in half?

About 420 more homes over 2027–2031 in the 50 places we have fees for, on top of about 449,000 that would be built anyway: 0.1% more. Even under the most generous assumptions it stays under 1%.

Why so few: fees are a sliver of what a home costs to build

For a typical new house in these places, coded fees are about $7,300, roughly 1.6% of the cost of building it. Halving them saves the builder about $1,800 once landowners take their share through land prices.

2. The answer is small under every assumption we can defend

The two numbers that matter most are how strongly builders respond and how much of the saving they keep. Pushing both to the edge of the published evidence:

"Most generous" takes the strongest response in the literature (1.75, a metro-wide figure), lets builders keep three-quarters of the saving, and phases the cut in twice as fast. "No response" is what several studies of impact fees find (Mayer and Somerville 2000; Campbell and Alm 2006).

CheckedThe fees. 928 fee rows from 59 places' published schedules, each with its source, converted to 2025 dollars.

The Handbook assumes fees seven times higher than these places charge

The challenge's benchmark starts from $50,000 a home and cuts $25,000. Real fees here are about $7,300. That is why the benchmark adds about six times as many homes as a realistic cut.

Raising fees by half would cost about as many homes

The same arithmetic runs in reverse: a rise of half the local fee loses about 410 homes over five years in these places.

Fees charged per unit weigh most on fourplexes

Most places charge water, sewer and impact fees for each dwelling, so a fourplex lot pays about three and a half times a house's fees. Per unit, that makes a cut matter about twice as much for duplexes and fourplexes as for houses. They are a small share of what gets built, so they add few homes in total.

Median fees per lot, MN, NC, OH and TX. Oregon's are four to five times higher: $34,000 for a house and $101,000 for a fourplex.

How this was estimated
  • Fees. Each place's coded fees for a house, a duplex and a fourplex (config/fees/), in 2025 dollars. Missing building-permit and water/sewer charges are filled from the median of coded places (D-065, D-066).
  • Homes without the cut. The baseline forecast for 2027–2031 by building size.
  • Effect of a cut. Central assumptions: builders keep half the saving (range 25–75%); building rises 0.3% for each 1% cut in cost (range 0 to 1.75); half the response shows within a year. Each building size uses its own fee and its own cost per unit. One Minnesota place with enough parcel data counts lots that newly pencil instead (D-032, D-073).
  • Which number leads. The headline uses the central assumptions. The likely range comes from 1,000 runs that draw every assumption. The range for the builder response is lopsided (0 to 1.75, peaking at 0.3), so the average run assumes a response of about 0.7 and adds about 850 homes; the page leads with the central value instead (D-073).
  • Not counted. Buildings of 5 or more units (no fees are coded for them), effects on prices or rents, approval times, and what governments do without the fee revenue.
  • Left out. Oregon (the transfer state) and the holdout places. Wilmington, Concord and Gastonia (NC) had no baseline or ACS value.
