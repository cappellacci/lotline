What happens if local governments cut their development fees in half?

About 1,500 more homes over 2027–2031 across Minnesota, North Carolina, Ohio and Texas, on top of about 1.6 million that would be built anyway: 0.1% more. Even under the most generous assumptions it stays under 1%.

Every place in each state, 2,937 in all. Below: the two worlds, why the effect is small, and why to believe it. The method is at the end.

Why so few: fees are a sliver of what a home costs to build

Where we have read the schedules, fees for a typical new house are about $7,300, roughly 1.6% of the cost of building it. Halving them saves the builder about $1,800 once landowners take their share through land prices.

Fees: 1.6%. In Oregon, where system development charges are high, fees are about 7% of the cost of a house.

2. The answer is small under every assumption we can defend

The two numbers that matter most are how strongly builders respond and how much of the saving they keep. Pushing both to the edge of the published evidence, all four states together:

"Most generous" takes the strongest response in the literature (1.75, a metro-wide figure), lets builders keep three-quarters of the saving, and phases the cut in twice as fast. "No response" is what several studies of impact fees find (Mayer and Somerville 2000; Campbell and Alm 2006).

The Handbook assumes fees far higher than these places charge

The challenge's benchmark starts from $50,000 a home and cuts $25,000. Real fees here are a few thousand dollars, so the benchmark adds about nine times as many homes as a realistic cut, and still under 1%.

Raising fees by half would cost about as many homes

The same arithmetic runs in reverse: a rise of half the local fee loses about 1,450 homes statewide over five years.

Fees charged per unit weigh most on fourplexes

Most places charge water, sewer and impact fees for each dwelling, so a fourplex lot pays about three and a half times a house's fees. Per unit, that makes a cut matter about twice as much for duplexes and fourplexes as for houses. They are about 3% of what gets built, so they add few homes in total.

Median fees per lot where we read the schedules, MN, NC, OH and TX. Oregon's are four to five times higher: $34,000 for a house and $101,000 for a fourplex.

How this was estimated
  • Places. Every place the baseline forecasts in each state (the Census building-permit reporting units, which do not overlap): MN 944, NC 221, OH 832, TX 940. They are the units the statewide baseline is summed over. Holdout places are inputs here: their later outcomes are filtered out and none is read.
  • Fees. Coded fees for a house, a duplex and a fourplex where we read the schedules (config/fees/), in 2025 dollars, with missing permit and water/sewer charges filled from coded places (D-065, D-066). Other places take their state's median coded fee.
  • Homes without the cut. The baseline forecast for 2027–2031 by building size.
  • Effect of a cut. Builders keep half the saving (range 25–75%); building rises 0.3% for each 1% cut in cost; half the response shows within a year. Each building size uses its own fee and its own cost per unit. Places with enough parcel data count lots that newly pencil instead (22 in Minnesota) (D-032, D-073).
  • The range. 1,000 runs draw every assumption. The builder response is drawn so its middle is 0.3, with a 1-in-20 chance above 1.75 (D-076); the earlier draw was lopsided and centred near 0.6. The middle estimate per state now matches the central-assumption calculation within a few percent.
  • Not counted. Buildings of 5 or more units (about 30% of expected homes; no fees are coded for them), effects on prices or rents, approval times, and what governments do without the fee revenue. Oregon (the transfer state) is not in this run.
  • Files. reports/fees-statewide-run/ (run log, totals, summary); scripts/dev/fee_dev_run.py --statewide; method in D-073 and D-076, branch ws/fees-phase2, PR #356.
