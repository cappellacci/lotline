Cutting fees adds few homes because fees are a small share of the cost of building

The fee scenario after fee plan Phase 2 (D-073): each building size takes its own dose, and a sensitivity raises fees instead of cutting them. 50 places with coded fees in Minnesota, North Carolina, Ohio and Texas; 1,000 draws each. Oregon and the holdout places are not in this run.

1

The Handbook's cut adds about 1% to the baseline; a realistic local cut adds about a fifth of a percent

Coded fees for a new house are about $6,500 to $9,200 a unit (2025 dollars), far below the Handbook's $50,000. Cutting them in half gives builders about $1,600 to $2,300 a home once landowners take their share. Each bar runs from the 10th to the 90th percentile of the five-year total (log scale); the table gives the medians.

2

Raising fees by half costs about as many homes as cutting them by half adds

The rise runs through the same land-price share, elasticity and phase-in as the cut. Where only the elasticity route is used (North Carolina, Ohio, Texas) it is the exact mirror. In Minnesota one place uses lots that pencil, and there a rise costs more homes (71) than the cut adds (57): lots near the threshold drop out faster than new ones cross it.

3

Per unit, duplexes and fourplexes respond about twice as much as houses, but few are built

The same fee per unit is a bigger share of a 1,000 sq ft unit's cost than of a 2,150 sq ft house's, so the plex dose is about 2.1 times the house dose. Plexes are a small part of the baseline (1 to 6%), and 5+ unit buildings, 34 to 83% of it, take no dose because no fees are coded for them. So houses still give most of the added homes everywhere; plexes add a visible share only in Ohio (39%) and Texas (22%, mostly Houston's duplexes).

5

Outside Oregon, fees are 1.5 to 3.6% of the cost of building

Fees over construction, fees and interest while building, median over places. Oregon's system development charges make it the exception (7 to 13%). This is why a fee cut moves feasibility so little: in most places the gap between what new homes sell for and what they cost is far larger than any fee. The land-value side of that comparison is weak for now: only 10 of 57 places have a new-home value from a parcel roll, and the rest use the ACS median home value, which covers older homes and understates new construction.
