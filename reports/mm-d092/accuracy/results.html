How accurate is the missing-middle model?

Honest, but not sharp. When we check the model against what was actually built, the real count lands inside its range about 8 times in 10, which is what a P10–P90 range should do. But the ranges are wide (often 10-fold), single-place medians can miss by 3–6×, and the data can't yet cleanly separate the reform's own effect from ordinary ups and downs in building. For a new state, our one real test, Oregon, came in inside the range with the median 1.3× too high.

Goal 1

Estimating missing-middle buildings in a place calibrated, wide

  • The ranges are honest. Across every test, 78% of 136 places land inside their P10–P90 range. The no-reform baseline alone, scored held out on 468 places it never saw, lands inside 79% of the time.
  • Single places are hard. The parcel-roll check passes (Wake, Franklin, Minneapolis all inside), but Franklin County built 142 against a median of 483 (3.4× too high) and Minneapolis built 275 against a median of 42 (6.5× too low), each just inside its range.
  • Why: 34–44% of 2–4 unit buildings come in projects of 3 or more (run 13). Whether one big project lands in a place in five years decides its count, and nothing in our data can foresee that.
  • Charlotte, our one blind test so far, passed: 119 built against 89–172 (median 104). That used the older D-055 values, not C4.
Goal 2

Projecting to a statewide total totals inside, except Minnesota

  • Adding places up tightens the range relative to the total, because one place's big project is offset by another's quiet year.
  • No-reform state totals: Texas built 4,515 (range 1,701–7,183) and Ohio 391 (45–1,266), both inside. Minnesota's three places built 32 against a top of 13: a miss, on small numbers (Rochester alone built 20).
  • Not yet tested directly: the statewide reform effect on its own. Every statewide number mixes the baseline (well tested) with the reform effect (weakly identified, below).
Goal 3

Predicting what a new state would build one real test, passed

  • Oregon held out. Fitted on the other states' 7 reforming places only, the model predicts Oregon's 86 places: median 915 (range 457–4,789) against 703 built. Inside, 1.3× high.
  • The other states can't serve as tests: each has 1–3 reforming places, and Minnesota's and Ohio's built none in the window.
  • The earlier blind Oregon forecast (V9), frozen with D-038's values, was inside its range (1,753 built, 1,738–6,469) but 1.5× high and beat by "no reform". That was an older model; C4 hasn't had a blind new-state test, because Oregon is now in its fit.
The weak spot

The reform's own effect isn't clearly detected weak

  • Held out, the model with a reform effect beats the same model with none by only 5.0 log points (SE 3.4), about 1.5 standard errors.
  • The placebo fails: moving each reform three years early gives a larger five-year rate (0.45 per 1,000 lots a year) than the real dates (0.27).
  • So the scenario's added homes rest on an effect the counts are consistent with but can't pin down. That is why θ's range spans about 150-fold, and why the tool shows wide ranges, not single numbers.
What would sharpen this

The next real test is St. Paul

St. Paul's blind forecast (V5, freeze due 17 November) would be C4's first blind test, if it is frozen after PR #436 merges. Beyond that, the honest limits stay: the outcome counts 2–4 unit buildings only (a floor for the Handbook's 2–6), and clustering means single-place forecasts will stay wide.
