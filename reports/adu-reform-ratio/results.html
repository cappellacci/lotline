When a city changed its ADU rules, did permits move the way the engine says?

The engine turns a place's own ADU rate into a reform rate by multiplying it by a ratio from the rules. We checked that ratio against the 10 places in 3 states whose coded rules changed while we were counting their ADUs: forecast the years after the change from the years before, with and without the ratio.

Reading: not shown either way. The ratio helps a little (log score +4.2, 2 SE 7.9), and the best scaling of it is ×1.3 (0.55–2.05): consistent with the engine's ratio and not with "rules don't matter", but ten events, half with no ADUs at all, can't show it. Nothing changes in the engine.

Only events with at least one ADU are shown. Raleigh and Greensboro moved up about as the engine says or more; Minneapolis fell by half when the engine expected almost no change; College Station and Burnsville barely moved. Small counts (Cornelius 2 → 4) are noise.

How much should the ratio count?

Each forecast's mean is the no-effect forecast × ratio^b. b = 1 is the engine as built; b = 0 means rule changes make no difference. The data put b at 1.3, with an interval from 0.55 to 2.05. With the refitted rule shares (each event's own place left out) it is 1.3 (0.55–2.20).

A coding mismatch the check turned up

In 6 of the 10 events, D-079's dose says the rules got looser while the engine's rule share says they got tighter. The clearest cases come from zoned coverage: Forest Lake's new rule is coded as covering about 6% of the city, Cottage Grove's 75%, while the rules before count as covering all of it. So "a newer, more specific rule" reads as a cut. This doesn't change the verdict, since those places built almost nothing, but the engine's ratio is only as good as the coding of before and after.

What this means

  • Keep the engine's ratio. Nothing contradicts it, and the one big reform (Raleigh) went further than it predicts, not less.
  • It is not proven either. Ten events, five with no ADUs, and short after windows. The ×2 claim in D-093 stays unchecked.
  • Worth fixing: rules coded with a zoned coverage after a change but none before read as a tightening. Checking how coverage is coded for old rules would make the ratio more trustworthy wherever it is used.
