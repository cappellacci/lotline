Does the ADU model hold up, and do its 2027–31 numbers look sane?

Two questions. First, when the model forecasts ADUs it then gets to check against, how close does it come? Second, what does it predict for each state from 2027, with today's rules and with the Handbook ADU bill? The last section lists what looks off.

1 · How well does it predict ADUs within a state?

Forecasting forward: fit on 2016–20, check against 2021–25

This is how the engine sets each counted place's no-reform rate: its own recent count, plus a "drift" allowance because places' rates change over time (D-091). Here each place's 2016–20 count forecasts its 2021–25 count.

forecast P10–P90, tick at P50◆ built (gold inside, purple outside)

The medians track the places well across three orders of magnitude, and the ranges are about the right width (D-091 targeted 80% inside). Minnesota's suburbs scatter most because they count only a handful each.

A state it has never seen

Each state is left out, the model is fitted on the others, and the left-out state's 20 largest places are predicted from their coded rules alone (D-096). This is the honest test for a new state.

Every state's actual count lands inside its range, but the ranges are wide (×5 to ×50), because without a place's own counts the model can't tell a busy city from a quiet one.

3 · What looks off

Places that allow no ADUs today get large predicted gains

ADUs a year per 1,000 eligible lots. Blue: the engine's Handbook prediction (mean over draws, 2027–31). Gold: what core cities that already allow ADUs actually built, 2021–25.

  • Dallas and Fort Worth carry 46% of Texas's added ADUs. Neither allows rentable ADUs today, so the engine has no own-count rate to scale and gives them the full fitted core-city rate (×5.3 a suburb's). Dallas comes out at about 2.3 a year per 1,000 lots: as high as Asheville, the busiest place we count, about twice Portland and three times Austin, all with permissive rules for years. Cleveland, in Ohio, is the same case. This is the biggest thing to look at: the core-city term is fitted on cities that already allow ADUs, mostly mature programs.
  • The no-reform rate runs above recent counts. In places with their own counts, the engine's 2027–31 mean is above their recent rate. Minnesota is ×2.9. Two reasons: the drift term's mean is 1.7× its median (the ranges are honest, but the mean is pulled up), and a half-count starting allowance on each of Minnesota's 184 counted places, most of which counted none. Medians sit closer to recent counts.
  • Oregon's added homes are tiny (about 10) because almost every Oregon lot already has a coded rule allowing ADUs (22% Handbook-equivalent, 74% allowed with some conditions not coded), so the bill changes little there. That is plausible after Oregon's 2017–19 state laws, but the 74% with uncoded conditions is worth a look.
  • Austin still gains under the bill even though its rule is Handbook-equivalent, because its zone list covers 64% of its lots (measured on parcels). The other 36% gain.
