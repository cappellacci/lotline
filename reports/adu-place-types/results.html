Lotline · D-088 ADU place types · 14 Nov 2026

Core cities build about three times as many ADUs per lot as suburbs, and that moves every held-out state toward its real count

D-088 asked where the differences between places sit (by state, by kind of place, or by how ADUs were counted) and whether a place type shared across states helps the model carry over to a state it has not seen. Typing places as core cities or suburbs gives the best leave-one-state-out result yet, and the pre-set rule adopts it over Form A. It adds nothing once owner share (Form A′) is in. And about 70% of the spread is between places of the same state, type and counting method, which no grouping reaches.

Types replace Form A; they do not improve Form A′

  1. Form A + types: adopted over Form A. tier-1 log score gain 7.2 > 2 SE (6.1); tests A-B 1 vs 1; test D -3.4 (SE 2.6).
  2. Form A′ + types: not adopted. no lower tier, and the tier-1 log score gain -0.6 is not above 2 SE (4.4).
  3. No form passes D-075's claim rule at any tier: that needs every scorable state useful, and Minnesota stays outside ×2 for every form.

Which pair counts depends on D-084, still open: keep Form A (then A + types is the form), or adopt A′ (then A′ stays as is). The two finalists, A + types and A′, are a near tie on the tier-1 log score (+7.2 and +6.8 over A); A + types has more useful states (3 against 1). That comparison is descriptive, not part of the rule. Nothing goes to config.

Finding 1

In this data, state, kind of place and counting method are almost the same thing

Before any fit, the 64 fit places split into 16 core cities, spread over all five states, and 48 suburbs, all in Minnesota and North Carolina. There are no towns. Texas, Oregon and Ohio contribute only core cities. Minnesota's 41 suburbs are all counted from the Met Council survey; the other states use city permit records or parsers. So "Minnesota is over-predicted" and "suburbs are over-predicted" and "survey places are over-predicted" are largely one statement, and Part 1 cannot pull them apart cleanly.

Finding 2

Each grouping explains about a quarter of the spread, and together not much more

Under Form A, kind of place explains 26% of the spread in place effects, state 23% and counting source 23%; all three together 29%. The pre-set reading is "types matter": type explains a little more than state, and core cities sit above suburbs with every state left out. A typical core city's effect is ×1.7, a suburb's ×0.3. Under Form A′, owner share has already absorbed most of it: the three explain 12%, 10% and 10% (reading: "mixed").

Form AForm A′ (owner share)

The rest, about 71% under A, is between places of the same state, type and counting method: Edina against Richfield, Cary against Durham. That is what no form so far can predict for a state it has not seen.

Finding 3

With types, every held-out state moves toward its observed count

Leave one state out, forecast it from the other four. Form A misses Texas ×5.9, Oregon ×3.6 and North Carolina ×2.7 low, and Minnesota ÷3.4 high. With the core-city term these become ×2.4, ×2.0, ×1.6 and ÷2.0: three of four states inside ×2 and their ranges. Owner share (A′) does about as well on Texas and less well elsewhere, and adding types to it changes little at tier 1.

Finding 4

The core-city effect is steady on its own, and mostly the same thing as owner share

On its own the core-city term holds at ×2.5 to ×3.5 with any state left out. Next to owner share it shrinks to about ×2.3, its interval includes no effect, and without Oregon it nearly vanishes. Core cities are where renters are (correlation with owner share -0.68). The two terms measure much the same urban-versus-suburban difference; types are simpler to explain and need only a metro map and a housing count.

Finding 5

Types don't help forecast the places the model has already seen

On D-085's test D (fit 2016–20, forecast 2021–25), types change nothing that matters: Minnesota is still forecast 8× too high, mostly Minneapolis's 2021 reform step. Carrying each place's own rate forward still scores best. Types answer "what will a new state look like", not "what will this city do next".
