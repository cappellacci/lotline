Missing-middle gut check

Do the app's missing-middle numbers hold up against what each state actually permitted in 2–4 unit buildings from 2019 to 2025? Yes. No state forecasts less with reform than it built recently, and none is out of line with history. One label in the app mixed up permits and net homes; that wording fix is in PR #474.

The grey bars all sit just left of the line: the no-reform forecast is close to, and a little under, recent building. The green bars show what the reform adds on top. 1 · What the numbers count

  • Period: a five-year total, 2027–2031. The added homes build up slowly: 2027 holds about 1% of the total and 2031 about 57%.
  • Added or total: added on top of the no-reform forecast.
  • Where: statewide. The Census permit survey places cover 97–99.9% of each state's permits.
  • Permits or homes: three measures, each smaller than the last. Extra permits → homes in place (finished, minus houses torn down to build them) → net new homes (minus a quarter that would have been built elsewhere in the state anyway). The app's headline is net new homes, about 40% of the extra permits.

The app's Method card asked how many homes "would be permitted", which doesn't match a headline that counts net homes. The ADU session has changed it to "how many more homes would it gain" in PR #474.

Baseline. The no-reform midpoints sit 4–22% under five years at the 2023–25 pace; Minnesota is lowest (2,182 against 2,807). The averages are within 11%. The yearly midpoint drifts down only because the spread of possible building cycles widens each year; the yearly averages stay flat. No state is far below its history.

Reform. The app's own check compares the with-reform midpoint, a year, with the 2023–25 average: every state passes (1.10 to 2.14). In Minnesota and North Carolina the added midpoint is about the state's whole recent five-year output. That fits St. Paul, which went from about 6 to 33 two-to-four unit buildings a year after its reform. Our blind test there said the model undershoots, not overshoots.

Newly legal lots and the share where building pays barely move between draws. Uptake ranges about 41-fold, from 1.2 to 48 homes per 1,000 lots where building pays, and carries almost all of the range. Its value is pooled, so it is the same in every state. A state's added homes next to its history depend on how many lots pencil out per home it already builds. That is about 660 in Minnesota, 594 in North Carolina, 334 in Ohio, 328 in Texas, 85 in Oregon.

One thing to know: the share where building pays has almost no spread (P10 to P90 within 2 points), so uncertainty about costs and prices is not adding to the range.

5 · One line per state

  • Minnesota: 1.27M newly legal lots × 29% where building pays × 0.8% built in five years ≈ 2,900 extra permitted homes (1,130 net), against about 2,200 without reform.
  • North Carolina: 2.47M newly legal lots × 25% where building pays × 0.8% built in five years ≈ 5,000 extra permitted homes (2,050 net), against about 5,100 without reform.
  • Ohio: 3.23M newly legal lots × 16% where building pays × 0.8% built in five years ≈ 4,200 extra permitted homes (1,750 net), against about 6,600 without reform.
  • Texas: 7.06M newly legal lots × 36% where building pays × 0.8% built in five years ≈ 20,200 extra permitted homes (7,970 net), against about 34,200 without reform.
  • Oregon: 197k newly legal lots × 28% where building pays × 0.8% built in five years ≈ 400 extra permitted homes (170 net), against about 2,700 without reform.

"0.8% built" is the median uptake: about 8 homes per 1,000 lots where building pays, over five years.
