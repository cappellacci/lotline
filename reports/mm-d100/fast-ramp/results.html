If builders took up the rule as fast as cities that chose it, five-year homes would be about 3.6 times higher

The main result keeps C4: the Handbook rule reaches half its full pace after 4 years, as Oregon's state-required places did. D-100 found that places which adopted the reform on their own reached half pace in about half a year. This sensitivity runs the same scenario with that fast ramp, changing nothing else: same θ, same draws, same seed.

Finding 1

The ramp is about timing: the fast ramp books in five years what C4 reaches later

C4, half-way 4.0 yearsfast ramp, half-way 0.5 yearsshare of the full (mature) rate in each scenario year

Both ramps reach the same mature rate. Over five years C4's ramp averages 23% of it and the fast ramp 84%, so the whole sensitivity is a multiplier of 4.20 ÷ 1.16 = 3.6 on every place. It moves no place relative to another and adds no new uncertainty: it shows how much of the five-year number depends on how quickly builders respond.

How to read it

An upper case for timing, not a second forecast

C4 stays the headline because the Handbook rule is a state law, and Oregon's state-required places ramped slowly. The fast ramp is fair only if a state law were taken up the way a city's own reform is: by a city that wanted it, often with builders already lined up (St. Paul built 55 buildings in two years). It also holds C4's θ; with R2's own θ (0.94 per 1,000) it would be about 9% lower. The locally adopted ramp rests on 37 places and 64 post-reform years and did not improve held-out forecasts.
